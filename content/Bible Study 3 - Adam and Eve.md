## Read through [Genesis 2.4-2.25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%202.4-2.25&version=NASB)
- Question: Big picture, what is happening in this story?
	- Just let them talk.
- Question: What are interesting things that you noticed?
	- Just let them talk.
- Question: We just read a creation account. Why is there another one that's different?
	- In [[Bible Study 2 - Creation#^creation-ideal|Bible Study 2 - Creation]] we discussed how [[Genesis 1.1-2.3]] functions as a summary of the bible and the ideal. This leaves the reader waiting to meet the real humans to see what they are like.
	- [[Genesis 1.1-2.3]] was focused on cosmic creation. This one is focused on humans. So here is the story the reader was expecting to get. The author decided that there was something important about the point of creation that was worth partially repeating to communicate to the reader.
	- You can reiterate that this is not just a video documentary of what happened. The authors have a purpose for writing and are using a story as the medium for which to communicate that. Sometimes they will skip things or retell things with a different focus to communicate a specific message to the reader.
		- Even after you know something, it takes time and work for it to set in mentally. Repetition helps with that.
-  Question: What does the passage say about the source of life?
	- Creation of man in verse 6 and 7
		- Water + dust = Clay
		- Then God formed the man, but he wasn't alive till God breathed into him.
	- God gives life both directly and indirectly.
		- Direct
			- God breathes life into the form of dust to make it a man.
			- "God caused every tree to grow" in verse 9.
		- Indirect
			- You can see in verse 5 that water from God in combination with man, who is also from God, results in plant life.
	- Referencing verses 10-14, 
		- Controlled water can be a source of life instead of representing chaos.
		- If you know a little about biblical geography you'll recall that good things don't come from Cush, Assyria, and Babylon (which is built next to the Euphrates).
			- God gives life to both the good and the wicked.
- Question: "Good" was a theme in the last story that gave us some clues to understand what was happening. Is there anything happening with "good" again?
	- Verse 9
		- Trees are good for food, but also pleasing to the sight. That's interesting.
			- In [[Genesis 1.1-2.3]], "God saw that it was good". So we have continued with the sight thing.
		- Verse 16 God's first command to Adam is to eat freely, basically to enjoy all these great trees I put here for you.
			- God wants Adam to enjoy his creation.
		- Verse 17, God tells man not to eat from the tree of knowledge of good and evil.
			- In [[Genesis 1.1-2.3]], God creates man to rule part of his creation. Knowing good from evil seems like it would be really helpful for that task.
			- What's up with the dying from eating fruit?
		- The Bible project uses the example of a dad giving his son a pocket knife. He says "Here is a gift, enjoy it! But don't stab your brother." A pocket knife can be a great thing, but one way to ruin that great thing is to stab your brother. [^2]
	- Verse 18
		- We have our first "not good" in the story.
- Question: Why is it "not good" for man to be alone? Both God and animals are there, so he isn't even technically alone.
	- [Genesis 1.27-28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%201.27-28&version=NASB)
		- God creates man in his image, but part of being the image of God requires the unity between multiple. Can't do that if you are the only one.
		- God's first command is to "be fruitful and multiply", and man cannot do that alone.
	- So it is "not good" because man literally cannot do or be who he was created to be without his counterpart, and there is nothing he can do with his own power to fix that.
- Question: We have encountered the first problem in the biblical story (ignoring chaos), and man cannot fix it. So how does God respond?
	- God creates a "helper", but to really understand it we'll have to start getting into the Hebrew.
		- The word in Hebrew is [ʿēzer (עֵזֶר)](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h5828/nasb20/wlc/0-1/).
		- The man doesn't need an assistant or someone to make his work easier. He physically is incapable of accomplishing his purpose without the helper.
		- So there is an element of salvation tied to this word.
			- This word is also used in [[Exodus 18|Exodus18.4]] where it says the help was my salvation.
- Some other Hebrew words that might be of interest:
	- Creation of Eve
		- Word for rib is commonly used for the side of a building or tent.
			- The word in Hebrew is [ṣēlāʿ](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h6763/esv/wlc/0-1/).
		- Uses a different word for creation of woman than when God created man.
			- The word in Hebrew is [bānâ](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h1129/nasb20/wlc/0-1/).
			- It is used for building cities or altars.
		- This is weird, even in Hebrew, and the authors did it on purpose. Though for now, just something to keep in the back of your mind.
	- Man placed in the garden
		- If you missed it, man was created outside the garden and placed inside in verse 8 and verse 15, but in verse 15 it uses a different word than 8.
			- For verse 15, the word in Hebrew is [yānaḥ](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h3240/esv/wlc/0-1/).
			- The [[The Pentateuch as a Narrative by John Sailhamer]] provides some insight into this:
			> ...in verse 15 he uses a term that he elsewhere has reserved for two special uses: God's "rest" or "safety" which he gives to human beings in the land (e.g., Ge 19:16; Dt 3:20; 12:10; 25:19), and the "dedication" of something before the presence of the Lord (Ex 16:33-34; Lev 16:23; Nu 17:4; Dt 26:4, 10) Both senses of the term appear to lie behind the author's use of the word in verse 15. The man was "put" into the Garden where he could "rest" and be "safe", and the man was "put" into the Garden "in God's presence" where he could have fellowship with God (3:8).
			> A second observation from verse 15 concerns the specific purpose for God's putting the man in the Garden. In most English translation of the verse the man is "put" in the Garden "to work it and take care of it." Although this interpretation is found as early as the LXX (2d century B.C.), there are serious objections to it. For one, [discussion of Hebrew noun genders that don't match]... Moreover, later in this same narrative (3:23), "working the ground" is said to be a result of the Fall, and the narrative suggests that the author has intended such a punishment to be seen as an ironic reversal of humanity's "working" and "keeping" the Garden would not provide a contrast to "working the ground." In view of these objections, which cannot easily be overlooked, a more suitable translation of the Hebrew text would be "to worship and obey." The man is put in the Garden to worship God and obey him. The man's life in the Garden was to be characterized by worship and obedience; he was to be a priest, not merely a worker and keeper of the Garden. Such a reading not only answers the objections raised against the traditional English translation but also suits the ideas of the narrative Throughout chapter 2, the author has consistently and consciously developed the idea of the human "likeness" to God along the same lines as the major themes of the Pentateuch as a whole-worship and sabbath rest. [^1]

			- This fits with the discussion in [[Bible Study 2 - Creation]] about the Garden being God's temple, and later in [[Exodus 19|Exodus 19.6]] where God tells the Israelites he is going to make them a kingdom of priests.
## Conclusions:
- Reminder: the bible isn't just a videography of events. The stories are more focused on the meaning of those events than the physical events themselves. So at times, the author will repeat things from different angles or skip things entirely to facilitate communicating with the reader.
- Question: How did this story expand our understanding of God?
	- Previously we discussed God being the source of life because he created everything. In this study we expanded that idea and added texture to it. 
		- Man was formed, but God has to breath life into him.
		- God giving life to everyone, good and evil.
	- Previously we discussed God delegating his power to rule with his creation. In this study we see God working indirectly, like through the river, to bless others and to bring his plan to fruition.
	- Previously we saw that God knows what is good. In this study we saw that God knows what is not good too.
- Question: How did this expand our understanding of ourselves?
	- Previously we discussed how we were created as God's image bearers. In this study we discussed how God created us to worship and obey him.
	- It also became clear that God is intent on us carrying out his purpose of filling the earth.
		- From [[Genesis 1.1-2.3]]
			- Void = chaos = bad
			- God commanded us to be fruitful and multiply to fill the earth.
- Question: How did this expand our understanding of our relationship to God?
	- So God gives life and blessings to everyone, both the good and evil, and allows them the opportunity to make choices about who they are going to be.
		- Reminder from [[Bible Study 2 - Creation]] that every human is created in God's image. He loves all of us. And all of us have the same potential inside of us to be the image bearers of God that he created us to be.
	- God created man, but he also created the means to which he is able to fulfill the commandments God gave him.
		- God could have just made more humans instead of tasking the man with making more humans, but God wants to work through us to see his purpose fulfilled.

		- Types of literature
		- Structure of the story
		- Repeated themes
		- Questioning things that seem off
		- Paying attention to details
		- Multiple translations
		- [BlueLetterBible.org](https://www.blueletterbible.org/)

	- Internalizing concepts requires dedicated time to think about.
