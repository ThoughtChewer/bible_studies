- Most Americans are familiar with this story.
	- The story in a nutshell:
		- Snake = evil.
		- Everything was perfect till the snake deceived Eve.
		- Then the humans ruined everything and we are now trying to get back to the garden.
	- But what if the story isn't as simple as that?
		- There is a physiological response when we encounter things that are familiar where our brains go into low-effort mode, which is more efficient, but leads to loss in attention to detail.
		- So lets look at some details and see what we find.
- Question: Are there any structural elements to the story that can help us?
	- The snake's contrast
		- 3.1: The serpent was more cunning than any animal of the field.
			- The Hebrew word for cunning is [ʿārûm (עָרוּם)](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h6175/nasb20/wlc/0-1/)
			- This is a morally neutral term. For example, it translated as prudent in Proverbs as a positive trait.
		- 3.14: Cursed are you... more than any animal of the field.
			- The Hebrew word for curse is ['ārar (אָרַר)](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h779/nasb20/wlc/0-1/)
		- The two lines are linked and differ in a word that sounds similar.
		- So you go from a complement about the snake being special to be especially cursed.
	- Naked and shame
		- The previous story ended with a weird line about being naked and not ashamed [Genesis 2.25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%202.25&version=NASB).
		- The first section of this story ends with Adam and Eve realizing that they were naked and hiding it (shame) in 3.7.
		- So clearly 2.25 is linking the two stories together and giving us some insight about the story instead of being a commentary on marriage.
	- Hebrew wordplay
		- The Hebrew word for naked in 2.25 is [ʿārôm (עָרוֹם)](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h6174/nasb20/wlc/0-1/)
		- The Hebrew word for naked in 3.7 is [ʿêrōm (עֵירֹם)](https://www.blueletterbible.org/lexicon/h5903/nasb20/wlc/0-1/)
		- The words used for naked and cunning have the same roots, so they both look and sound the same.
	- Tying it together
		- The story is about a fall. Not just for humans, but for the snake too.
			- Recall in [[Bible Study 2 - Creation]] that we discussed humans are not the only storyline in the bible.
		- The fall has something to do with being crafty/prudent/wise (as Eve describes the fruit in 3.6).
- Question: The story is using parallel and contrasting imagery to communicate to us, and it has linked itself both to the previous story with 2.25 and to [[Genesis 1.1-2.3]] with the [[Bible Study 2 - Creation#^hebrew-minimal| imagery of fruit trees, humans, and seeds.]] Are there any other ideas we can connect?
	- Seeing good and not good
		- God
			- "and God saw that it was good" repeatedly in [[Genesis 1.1-2.3]]
			- God caused trees to grow that were pleasing to sight and good for food in Genesis 2.9
			- God said, "It is not good for man to be alone" in Genesis 2.18
			- God created the tree of the knowledge of good and evil in 2.9
		- Eve
			- Saw that the tree was good...and that the tree was desirable to make one wise.
		- This points to a discrepancy between sources for information. Other examples include:
			- God tells Adam one thing about the trees, but Eve tells the snake something different (2.16-17 vs 3.2-3).
				- Adam communicated the message to Eve incorrectly (implied)
			- Snake says you'll be like God in 3.5, but in 1.26 God makes mankind in his own image and likeness.
	- Creation and harmony vs death and discord
		- The pair of stories in Genesis 2-3 start with the creation of life and harmony between Adam and animals and Adam and Eve.
		- The stories end with Adam and Eve hiding parts of their bodies from each other, the animosity between humanity and the snake, the death of an animal to clothe Adam and Eve, and the (eventual) death of Adam and Eve.
			- God is the source of life, he had to breath into Adam's form before Adam came to life, and banishment from that source will result in eventual death.
- Question: In America, gaining knowledge and understanding is basically always viewed as a positive thing. Was it a positive thing for Adam and Eve?
	- The first thing that happened is they realized they were naked and hid their bodies from the other instead of becoming one flesh.
	- The second thing they did was hide from God instead of walking with him.
	- The third thing they did was blame someone else for their actions which is never a good trait in a ruler.
		- Adam blames his "Helper", aka salvation from God as discussed in [[Bible Study 2 - Creation]], as the source of his problems.
	- These all seem like they are further from the ideal that God created them to be.
- Question: Was it really perfect to start with?
	- Reminder: [[Genesis 1.1-2.3]] is the ideal and [[Genesis 2.4-2.25]] starts the story with the characters based in reality.
	- Then right off the bat things were not ideal.
		- Adam was created outside of the garden, aka outside of the Temple, which is where heaven and earth meet, aka outside of God's direct presence. And then God creates the garden as a place that they can be together, and places Adam in it.
		- Even after that Adam is incapable of fulfilling his duty as an image bearer because he was alone.
		- The fall wasn't only for the humans. The snake chose to rebel too.
	- So the story is not: everything was perfect and then two humans ruined it for the rest of us.
	- "The biblical story is: God created a space for his humans to partner with the divine project to spread life and order out of the realm of chaos and non-order... It is an ideal that was never realized"[^1]

	- "Although much can be said about the curse on the snake, the woman, and the man, it is important to note that the text says very little. In this passage we can see most clearly the artful composition that produced the Pentateuch. There are no long discourses on the appearance of the snake before and after the curse. Did he have feet? Did he have wings? The thoughts of the snake, if there were such, or the thoughts of the man and woman are left completely out of view. The narrative gives nothing to help us understand their plight as individuals. The snake, the woman, and the man are not depicted as individuals involved in a personal crisis; rather, they are representatives. We are left with the impression that this is not their story so much as it is our story, the story of humankind. With great skill the author has present these three participants as the heads of their race. The snake on the one hand and the man and the woman on the other are as two great nations embarking on a great struggle, a struggle that will find its conclusion only by an act of some distant and as yet unidentified "seed.""[^2]

- “Although it is assumed by all the English translations that ערום has a negative sense (‘crafty’) in Genesis 3:1, a closer examination suggests otherwise. The description of the serpent commences with its being ‘more prudent (_‘arum_) than all the creatures of the field’ (Genesis 3:1), and after having tempted Eve, concludes with its bing ‘more cursed (_’arur_) than all the creatures of the field (3:14).’ The two lines are nearly identical in Hebrew, suggesting an intentional contrast between them: the cursed serpent is a negative contrast to an initially positive shrewd serpent. Not only does ‘prudent’ make more sense of the narrative flow of events, it also distances God from any responsibility with respect to the origin of evil. God did not make a ‘crafty’ creature; he made a wise creature. The serpent’s ‘prudence’ may even be a sign of God’s special favor toward the serpent above the other animals. The serpent’s decision to use its prudence for evil intentions, however, resulted in a fall from divine favor to eternal humiliation, and this offers a solution to the age-old question of the serpent’s (and Satan’s) fall. When did the serpent rebel and fall? It ‘fell’ in Genesis 3. Thus, Genesis 3 depicts the fall of Adam, and Eve, and the serpent.” Postell, Seth (2011). Adam as Israel. Pickwick Publications.
